# InsecureShort (#31 "See People's Insecurities") — fact check
Checked by web search 2026-11-15; some pages secondary. Liking-gap bars are schematic (no values claimed). "Months" calendar = illustration of "lasted for months", not exact months.

| Claim | Source | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 1978: psychologists described high achievers who felt like frauds; named it the impostor phenomenon | Clance & Imes, "The impostor phenomenon in high achieving women: Dynamics and therapeutic intervention", Psychotherapy: Theory, Research & Practice 15(3):241–247, 1978 | clinical interviews with 150+ high-achieving women; "phenomenon", not a diagnosis |

| 2003: nervous speakers rated their own visible nervousness higher than observers did (5.71 vs 4.24 of 10) | Savitsky & Gilovich, "The illusion of transparency and the alleviation of speech anxiety", J. Exp. Soc. Psych. 39:618–625, 2003 (Study 1) | numbers from secondary summary of Study 1 |
| After conversations with strangers people underestimate how much they were liked ("liking gap"); among new dorm mates it lasted for months | Boothby, Cooney, Sandstrom & Clark, "The liking gap in conversations", Psychological Science 29(11):1742–1756, 2018 | 5 studies; gap shrank late in the year |
| 2018: people judge their own showing of vulnerability as weakness, the same act by others as courage ("beautiful mess effect"), 7 studies | Bruk, Scholl & Bless, "Beautiful mess effect", JPSP 115(2):192–205, 2018 | mostly imagined scenarios; status can reverse it (Gibson, Harari & Marr 2018) |
